- A dragon guards the entrance to the Watts Chapel. Its image is carved into an enormous set of oak doors, where tangled limbs and a menacing grin are pierced by an iron cross. Above, a grand arch features angelic faces that alternate between looking up in hope and down in sorrow. This miracle in terracotta stretches across the exterior of the building, where ancient symbols of hope, liberty and spirituality adorn buttresses, capitals and columns.
- This glorious entryway is just a foretaste of the treasures that await inside. The glittering interior is an ode to heaven and earth, as richly decorated as the pages of an illuminated manuscript. Crimson-covered seraphs and cherubic faces stretch into the curves of a magnificent dome, while below, angels clutch discs that depict the duality of human existence, all of which are knotted together among the blooming vines of a Tree of Life.
- This astonishing Arts and Crafts building was the brainchild of Mary Watts, wife of Symbolist artist George Frederic Watts. After the couple moved into their new Surrey home of Limnerslease in 1891, she soon went about conceiving a place of spiritual contemplation for their local community, specifically a mortuary chapel, where loved ones could attend to the dead before burial.

- Mary Watts was no stranger to painting or sculpture, having trained at the Slade School of Fine Art. There her deft ability with clay had been evident, yet the chapel offered her the opportunity to flex her creative muscles as never before. She set about manifesting the signs of ancient spiritual languages - from Egyptian hieroglyphs to Celtic crosses - into a single, spectacular space. In a 1905 essay explaining the nuances of these complex decorations, she writes: 'A symbol may well be compared to a magic key. In one hand it is nothing more than a piece of quaintly wrought iron, in another it unlocks a door into a world of enchantment.'
- Contemporary sculptor Halima Cassell remembers the first time she experienced this world, visiting the chapel in preparation for an exhibition at the Wattses' purpose-built art gallery. 'It was truly astonishing,' she says. 'I have always been interested in religious buildings, but this was the first time I felt an overwhelming sense of femininity and warmth. Mary Watts's use of pattern, craftsmanship and inclusion of symbolism from different cultures gives the building an incredible sense of harmony.' Your World of Interiors
