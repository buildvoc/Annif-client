- Willmer House is a Grade I listed Georgian townhouse located at 38 West Street in Farnham, Surrey, England, renowned for its exceptional early 18th-century architecture and multifaceted historical roles. Constructed in 1718 by local hop merchant and maltster John Thorne as a grand display of wealth, the building features a fine red brick facade with elaborate gauged brickwork, rusticated Doric pilasters, and a bracketed cornice, contrasting with simpler construction at the rear. [1] [2] [1]
- Originally built on the site of an earlier Elizabethan structure, Willmer House passed through various owners after Thorne's bankruptcy in 1738, including brewer Samuel Slater, who altered its grounds in the mid-18th century. In 1809, it was acquired by Anne Willmer, who named it after her family and established a school for young ladies; this was later repurposed as a boys' boarding school under Thomas Hunter from 1842 to 1860. The property then served as a private residence, a dental surgery under James Sidney Longhurst from 1876 to 1924, and temporary quarters for a Canadian Army unit during World War II, reflecting its adaptability amid Farnham's evolving social and economic landscape. Since 1961, when acquired by Waverley Borough Council and converted into the Museum of Farnham, it has housed collections on local history from prehistoric times through the town's hop-growing era and Georgian prosperity, alongside an art and craft library. Its interiors preserve notable 18th-century elements, including a fine contemporary staircase, panelled rooms, and fireplaces. Restoration works began in January 2025, including facade repairs, timber roof structure replacement, and structural preservation, funded by grants from Arts Council England, the National Lottery Heritage Fund, and local councils, along with public fundraising, with completion expected in 2026. - This Baroque-style exterior is renowned for its craftsmanship, described in the Pevsner Architectural Guide as one of the finest cut-brick façades in England. Architectural historian Nathaniel Lloyd praised the Doric-order brick pilasters in his 1929 work A History of English Brickwork as perhaps the most beautiful extant example. The building contributes to the group value of listed structures along West Street, enhancing the street's historic character. [4] [5] [1]
- The interior of Willmer House showcases early 18th-century Georgian craftsmanship, consistent with the building's overall Baroque style. The hall features an elaborate Ionic pilaster supporting an entablatured archway, while a secondary doorway separating the front and back rooms is framed by Corinthian pilasters and decorative carved spandrels. A fine oak staircase rises through the house, complemented by extensive period wood panelling and original fireplaces that highlight the period's attention to detail. These elements contribute to the house's reputation as one of Farnham's finest examples of domestic architecture, as noted in Exploring Museums: The Home Counties (1990) by Nell Hoare and Geoffrey Marsh. - History and Ownership
- Construction and Early Use
- Willmer House was constructed in 1718 on the site of the former Elizabethan-era Arundel's House at 38-40 West Street in Farnham, Surrey, for John Thorne, a prosperous local maltster and hop merchant. Thorne commissioned the demolition of the existing structure and the erection of a new townhouse to reflect his wealth derived from Farnham's thriving hop trade, which supplied much of England's brewing industry during the early 18th century. The building exemplifies Baroque style with elaborate red-brick gauged work on its façade, a technique involving hand-cut bricks to create smooth, decorative surrounds that was fashionable among affluent merchants of the period. [2] [2] [10]
- From its completion, Willmer House served primarily as Thorne's private residence, underscoring the opulence of Farnham's merchant class and their ties to the hop-growing economy. The property included an adjacent brewhouse, now the site of the museum's modern annexe, which supported Thorne's malting and trading activities. This early configuration highlighted the house's role as both a family home and a symbol of [2] [2]
- commercial success in the local community.

- 19th- and 20th-Century Changes
- In the early 19th century, Willmer House underwent significant adaptation for educational purposes, reflecting the growing demand for private schooling in Farnham. By 1809, the property had been acquired by Anne Willmer, whose daughters established it as Willmer House School for Young Ladies, a boarding institution accommodating up to nine pupils aged 10 to 15. This venture operated until 1842, when Thomas Hunter, previously running a boys' school nearby, purchased the house and repurposed it as a larger boarding school for boys, with the 1851 census recording ten male pupils aged 11 to 18 residing there. These changes highlight Farnham's evolving social landscape, where affluent families sought specialized education amid the town's prosperity as a market center. [2] [2] [11]
- Following the closure of Hunter's school around 1860, Willmer House reverted to private residential use for about 16 years, during which it saw restorations to restore its original domestic layout. Leased to various owners, including Mrs. Caroline Paine in 1866-who also owned the adjacent Sandford House-the property underwent modifications such as the demolition of an intervening brewhouse and the addition of service annexes, alongside raising the garden wall for enhanced privacy. In 1876, former pupil James Sidney Longhurst transformed the house into a dual-purpose dental surgery and private residence, a role it fulfilled until 1924; contemporary accounts from his grandson describe the drawing room as off-limits during procedures and the surgery itself as foreboding, underscoring the building's shift to professional medical use amid Farnham's expanding healthcare needs. [2] [2]
- The 20th century brought further institutional changes, particularly during wartime. After Longhurst's tenure, the house passed through private hands until 1939, when, following the death of owner Mrs. Burke, it was bequeathed to her sister in Scotland; its contents were stored, and the empty building was lent to a unit of the Canadian Army stationed in Farnham during World War II, serving temporary military quarters from 1939 to 1945. Following the war, the house remained in private ownership, including by Canon J.M.C. Crum, until offered to public authorities in the late 1950s. While still privately owned, Willmer House was designated a Grade I listed building on 26 April 1950 by the Ministry of Works, acknowledging its outstanding Georgian architectural qualities and intact interior features, [2]
